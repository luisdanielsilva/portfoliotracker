Buy the dips. Sell near the tops. On a rule, not a feeling.

Ten shares at €100, then ten more at €70, and your average cost is €85 — the stock only has to climb back to €85 to put you in profit, not €100. Portfolio Tracker keeps that number current for every holding and works from it in both directions: it emails you when a stock falls under your cost, when you are up 75% on what you actually paid, and when a holding is 20% off its own 12-month high — so you act on your own plan instead of noticing three weeks late.

It won't call the bottom or the top. It makes sure you don't miss your own signal.

How it works

One thing you type in, then three that happen without you — the same four steps whether the price is falling or running. Blue is you; green runs on a schedule; red is the moment it interrupts you.

  1. The day you trade You

    Register the transaction

    Ticker, quantity, what you paid, the currency, and the exchange rate you actually got. This is the only thing you ever type in — every number on every chart is derived from it.

  2. Once a day, market shut Automatic

    The closing prices come in

    A job wakes while the US market is closed, takes the latest close for every ticker you hold, converts it to euros, and recomputes your average cost, market value and drawdown. Weekends are skipped.

  3. The moment a rule is met Alert

    You get told — once

    Every rule is re-checked against the fresh prices — against your average cost, against your target, against the holding's own 12-month high — and a crossing sends an email. Everything that fired arrives as one message, and each rule fires at most once in 24 hours, so neither a long slide nor a long run floods your inbox.

  4. Your call You

    Decide, with the number in front of you

    The email carries the figure the decision turns on: how far under your average the price has fallen, how far above it you are, or how far the stock has come off its own high. Whether you add, trim or sit still is entirely yours to judge — the job here was making sure you knew while it still mattered.

How the signal is built, and why it is built that way

Ranking the price against its own history

Three trailing windows are used: 6 months, 1 year and 2 years. For each one, today’s close is given a percentile rank against every close inside that window — a rank of 90 means the price has been lower than this on 90% of those days. Ties count as half, and the day itself is part of its own window.

Each rank becomes a regime: Strong High at 95 or above, High at 80, Low at 20 or below, Strong Low at 5 or below, and Neutral in between. A Strong reading is worth 2, a plain one 1, Neutral 0.

The two lanes, and why Buy is looser than Sell

Sell needs two of the three windows to read High, in both lanes. Buy needs two windows in the Confirmed lane, but only one in the Early lane — and that asymmetry is the whole point of running two lanes at once.

The reason is in how a real dip appears. A sentiment-driven fall shows up first in the shortest window, while the 1-year and 2-year windows are still anchored to a prior run-up and read Neutral all the way down. Requiring two-window agreement on Buy was found, in testing, to miss genuine pullbacks entirely — a roughly 14% drawdown in which the 6-month window read Strong Low for the entire decline while the longer two never budged. The Early lane catches that; the Confirmed lane tells you whether anything broader agrees. Neither is the right answer on its own, which is why you get both.

Confidence is the summed weight of the agreeing windows over the maximum possible (2 × 3 = 6), so a single plain Low reads 17% and all three Strong reads 100%. Under 25% is Watch, then Signal, Strong, and Very Strong at 75% and above.

A 5-year window was tried and dropped

It sat in the High zone 88% of the time and never once reached Low, because it was anchored to a crash low several years old rather than to anything resembling current conditions. Adding one back would bias the whole tool toward Sell.

Why it calls a winner “expensive” so often

Ranking a price level means a stock in a long climb sits near the top of its own history most days — so the sell side reads high for a holding that is doing well. Across these holdings it says sell on about 44% of days, and on 76% of them for the strongest performer.

That looks like a flaw, and the obvious repair is to rank how far the price has strayed from its own moving average instead, which removes the bias entirely. It was tried, at four different averaging lengths. It does balance the readings — and it takes the usefulness with it: the gap between the days it rates highest and lowest collapses from about 10 percentage points to under 3. The lopsidedness is not a defect sitting on top of the signal; in this data it is most of the signal. What the score is really detecting is closer to a trend than to a stretched elastic band, and it is more honest to say so than to tune it until it looks even.

What it does not know

Nothing here has any idea what the company is worth. There are no earnings, no revenue, no news and no volume in this — only closing prices. A price can be statistically low because the market is wrong, or because it is right and something has genuinely broken, and no arrangement of these numbers can tell the two apart.

Alerts from this signal

This one is not a rule you build. It watches every holding, always, and it emails about exactly one thing.

It emails you when a holding reads very strong buy — the deepest green in the strips above, which needs all three windows to agree the price is unusually low. Across these holdings that is roughly five emails a year for the whole portfolio.

It never emails you to sell. A sell reading is not an event: it is true on 39% of all days here, and 76% of days for the best performer — whose sell days were then followed by a 13.8% rise over the next three months. An alert that is usually true, and loudest where it is most wrong, teaches you to ignore your inbox. Selling is what the target alert is for, on the Alerts tab: it fires off what you actually paid, so it can only happen once.

Instead, the sell side turns up in a weekly summary every Monday, listing where each holding stands. It is a list, not a demand — nothing in it needs doing.

How to read this

  • Portfolio over time chart — Shows your portfolio’s market value over time. Use EUR or USD to toggle currency (Y-axis scale stays proportional). Select time periods (1M, 3M, 6M, 1Y, etc.) to zoom into specific ranges. X-axis shows quarters for longer periods or months for shorter periods. Vertical lines mark stock split events.
  • Stock splits — Marked with a vertical line on the chart. Events listed below the chart show the date, ticker, and split ratio (e.g., 3-for-1). Your share count automatically adjusts to reflect the split.
  • Series selection — Pick any combination of “Total”, “Tesla”, “Ex-Tesla”, or individual holdings to plot. The chart updates instantly. Colored dots mark points where shares were purchased.
  • Share counts table — Toggle between Amount (cost basis in original currency) or Shares held to see quantities. Values show in USD for stocks bought in USD (TSLA, AMD) or EUR for others. Collapsible to reduce clutter.
  • Original currency — Each stock displays in its purchase currency: TSLA and AMD in USD (using the transaction’s exchange rate), others in EUR. This shows your actual cost basis.
  • Events timeline — Below the chart, corporate actions like stock splits are listed chronologically by date, ticker, and event type.
